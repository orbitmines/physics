**met(R) = \frac{1}{c̄^(D-2)}·\paren{\frac{ln(R/c̄)}{R^(2D-1)·core} + 2·\frac{1}{R^(D-1)}}**

| lattice | D | DEG | box | ticks |

#### the derivation

**met(R) = met_far·(1 + near)**  
<sub>definition · met.integral</sub>  

so the whole of it is the long-range law times one plus a correction. The one is what becomes Newton's inverse square; the correction is the middle's logarithm over the long-range term, and it carries c̄/R - worth per cent at a few cells and nothing at all once R is an astronomical number of lattice steps

**met_far = 2 · core**  
<sub>definition · met.integral</sub>  

there is a core at each end and they are the same by symmetry. Added to the inverse-square part of the middle, the (R - c̄) that each carries cancels over a common denominator - which is what makes the long-range law a plain power of R with no trace of the core size left in its exponent

**met(R) = 2·near·core + 2·core**  
<sub>derived · substituting</sub>  

```
met(R) = met_far + met_far·near
met_far = 2·core
met(R) = 2·near·core + 2·core
```

met_far is not a primitive of this theory - it is 2·one core, so it stands in for itself here and the result is multiplied out

**near = \frac{middle}{met_far}**  
<sub>definition · met.integral</sub>  

the correction is what the middle's tail is worth against the long-range law - a ratio of the two, and the thing that goes to nothing as R grows

**near = \frac{middle}{met_far}**  
<sub>derived · as a ratio</sub>  

near is the middle over met_far, which written out is \frac{the middle}{met_far} - kept in the counts rather than worked out, so that whatever uses it can cancel against them

**met(R) = 2·\frac{core·middle}{met_far} + 2·core**  
<sub>derived · substituting</sub>  

```
met(R) = 2·near·core + 2·core
near = \frac{middle}{met_far}
met(R) = 2·\frac{core·middle}{met_far} + 2·core
```

near is not a primitive of this theory - it is \frac{the middle}{met_far}, so it stands in for itself here and the result is multiplied out

**core = \int_0^(c̄) integrand dx**  
<sub>definition · met.integral</sub>  

one core runs from the body out to a distance of one core, and across it the integrand is flat - so its contribution is that value times the width

**integrand = c̄^(-D+1)·R^(-D+1)**  
<sub>definition · met.integral</sub>  

inside the core at one end the near density has flattened off at its cap - a shell is never smaller than the cell its source sits in - while the far density barely moves across a width of one core. So the integrand there is the product of the two, neither depending on x any more. How fast each thins is the dense regime's: there are partners everywhere, so the hand-off is never what a carrier waits for and the lattice's one-cell-a-tick is the binding bound

**core = \frac{1}{R^(D-1)·c̄^(D-2)}**  
<sub>derived · integrating a power</sub>  

```
core = \int_0^(c̄) \frac{1}{R^(D-1)·c̄^(D-1)} dx
= \frac{x^(1)}{1}, at the limits
= \frac{1}{R^(D-1)·c̄^(D-2)}
```

the integrand in a core is \frac{1}{R^(D-1)·c̄^(D-1)}, a power 0 of x. Integrated that is x^(1)/1, taken between 0 and c̄

**met(R) = 2·\frac{1}{R^(D-1)·c̄^(D-2)} + 2·\frac{middle}{R^(D-1)·c̄^(D-2)·met_far}**

one core is not a primitive of this theory - it is \frac{1}{R^(D-1)·c̄^(D-2)}, so it stands in for itself here and the result is multiplied out

**middle = \int_c̄^(R) tail dx**  
<sub>definition · met.integral</sub>  

the middle runs from the edge of one core to the edge of the other, and one over x integrated between them is a logarithm - which grows more slowly than any power, and is the whole of why the correction dies away

**tail = R^(-D)·\frac{1}{x}**  
<sub>definition · met.integral</sub>  

between the two cores the integrand is a genuine product of two falling densities. By partial fractions that splits into a part carrying the same power of the separation as the cores do - which sums with them - and this part, one power of R further down and going as one over the distance along the line. It is the second that gives the correction, and being a power lower is exactly why the correction shrinks as the bodies are moved apart

**middle = \frac{ln(R/c̄)}{R^(D)}**  
<sub>derived · integrating a power</sub>  

```
middle = \int_c̄^(R) \frac{1}{x} dx
= ln(R/c̄)
```

the middle's tail goes as one over x, whose integral is a logarithm - so between c̄ and R it is ln(R/c̄). It grows more slowly than any power of x, which is what matters about it here

#### what the runs found

`counts/what-the-tiling-fixes` - holds. fcc-12: D = 3, DEG = 12, SHEET = 6, CYCLE = 6 - counts, not measurements

- D = 3 - how many dimensions fcc-12 has
- DEG = 12 - the ways out of a point on fcc-12 - the exits it has, counted
- SHEET = 6 - the exits with no component along the pulsing axis, which is what a source emits over. NOT DEG, which is 12
- CYCLE = 6 - how many steps go round the ring of a turn on fcc-12
- slice = 2 - a turn sweeps a PLANE, and a plane needs two dimensions - so what a turn lives in is min(D, 2), which is 2 here and stops growing at 2 however many dimensions are added
- c̄ = 1 - a step's worth of lean is what one annihilation buys a path, and a step is c̄ - one cell a tick, the lattice's own speed. Its value in those units is one, which is a statement about the units and not about the quantity

`lattice/what-the-tiling-is` - holds. the ball is a polynomial of degree 3 in r̄ (D is 3), checked exactly by finite differences; ρ = 1.0000 per unit volume; the exit set is isotropic at second order

- volume of one fundamental cell = 1 - |det basis| for fcc-12, by elimination in 3 dimensions - from the basis vectors themselves, with no run and no fit
- sites within r̄ steps = 8 - walked over the lattice's own exits from the centre: 1, 13, 55, 147, 309, 561, 923, 1415, 2057 for r̄ = 0, 1, 2, ...
- degree of the count in r̄ = 3 - the 3-th difference of the counts is constant at 20, and a sequence whose 3-th difference is constant is a polynomial of degree 3. Integer arithmetic - ball(r̄) = 1, 13, 55, 147, 309, 561, 923, 1415, 2057  |  Δ^(1) = 12, 42, 92, 162, 252, 362, 492, 642  |  Δ^(2) = 30, 50, 70, 90, 110, 130, 150  |  Δ^(3) = 20, 20, 20, 20, 20, 20
